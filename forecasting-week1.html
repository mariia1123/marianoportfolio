Forecasting Methods & Performance

Supply Chain Forecasting, BMME075, February 10, 2026

SoloUK exportsMoving averagesHolt LES
View PDF

Monthly UK exports to the EU from January 2018 to early 2026, 94 observations covering the 2020 COVID-19 slump and the Brexit transition rules from 2021. The question of the week: among naive, moving averages of different windows, and Holt's linear exponential smoothing, which simple method actually does best on a series like this?

Q1

What does the series look like?

Exports drift around a stable level through 2019, then collapse during the 2020 COVID-19 disruption, the lowest values in the plot. After that, they rebound quickly, dip again with Brexit's new rules-of-origin checks in 2021, and grow steadily until mid-2023 before leveling off. Trade in goods with the EU fell 23.1% in 2020, and remains roughly 10-15% below the pre-Brexit counterfactual.

Q2

Are year-over-year changes more volatile than month-to-month?

Yes, clearly. Standard deviation, range, and the 25-75 percentile spread are all wider for yearly relative changes than for monthly ones, by roughly 65% across the IQR. So data spread over more time turns out to be more volatile, not less.

Q3

When did volatility peak?

Running coefficients of variation at 6, 12, and 18-month windows all spike during 2020 and stay high through early 2022, exactly the COVID and Brexit transition window. After mid-2022, however, the 12 and 18-month CVs drop, suggesting the series stabilizes as firms adjust to the new rules.

Q4

Does Holt's smoothing learn the same parameters across periods?

No, the estimates jump around. On the full sample, the level adapts quickly (α = 0.37) but the trend is essentially zero. The 2020-2022 sub-period is similar with a smaller alpha (0.19). In 2023 onward, smoothing becomes very heavy (α = 0.04) and reacts slowly. In other words, the series has no consistent trend signal, the level is the only thing that's really changing.

Q5

Which forecast wins on the 12-month holdout?

Moving averages, by a wide margin. MA(12) takes the lowest RMSE (814) and MAPE (1.19%), with errors growing as the window shortens. Naive and Holt LES both produce RMSE above 5,500, since they overreact to recent observations in a series that has no real trend.

RMSE on the 12-month holdout, by method
Naive and Holt LES both overreact to short-term moves, so their bars run far past the moving averages.

Supply Chain Note

For high-noise, low-trend trade data, the choice of window matters more than the choice of method. A 12-month moving average works because it smooths short-term noise while staying responsive to actual level shifts. As a result, sophistication has a cost when the underlying signal is weak.
