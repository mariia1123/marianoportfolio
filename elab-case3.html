Portfolio Optimization with CAPM

Group of 4 · RStudio · ELAB Case III · June 2023

Group of 4Portfolio theoryCAPMCross-validationSharpe ratio
View PDF

Goal

Build mean-variance optimal portfolios for a basket of stocks, then test how much CAPM helps when estimating expected returns. The whole exercise sits inside a cross-validation loop so we can see how each approach holds up out of sample.

Setup

Pulled daily prices for our team's ticker list (group 1C) from Yahoo Finance using quantmod, computed log returns from 2003 onwards, and split the daily observations into two folds: odd days and even days. One fold trains the portfolio, the other validates it, and then we swap.

Approach

  • Means and variances: estimate on both folds, compare via scatterplot. Most points sit on the y=x line, so the estimates are reasonably stable across subsamples.
  • Mean-variance optimization: solve for portfolio weights with no short-selling and a target return, using solve.QP from quadprog.
  • CAPM layer: compute each stock's beta against SPY, then use CAPM-implied means (rf + β·(market return − rf)) as inputs to the same optimizer.
  • Shrinkage: add κ·I to the covariance matrix and sweep κ over {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1} to find the value that maximizes out-of-sample Sharpe.

Results

The headline finding is that CAPM-implied means produce much steadier portfolios than raw historical means. With sample means, Sharpe ratios swing between in-sample and out-of-sample as the optimizer overfits noise. With CAPM means, the Sharpe ratios stay tight and high across both folds.

Sharpe ratios (no short-selling):
Sample means, in-sample 0.79 and 0.57, out-of-sample 0.55 and 0.77
CAPM-implied means, in-sample 1.22 and 1.04, out-of-sample 1.04 and 1.22

SPY ends up at roughly 5.75% weight in the no-shortselling portfolio, which is lower than theory might suggest but consistent with the optimizer preferring diversification across the individual names rather than concentrating in the market index.

Beta against CAPM-predicted returns for the odd and even subsamples. Each fold lines up neatly along its own Security Market Line, which is the visual proof that CAPM imposes structure where raw sample means show none.

Shrinkage Sweep

Sweeping κ from 0.2 to 1 made very little difference for the sample-means portfolio. Validation Sharpe nudged up from 0.553 at κ=0.2 to 0.554 at κ=1, so full shrinkage (κ=1) was nominally optimal, but the dataset wasn't really noisy enough for shrinkage to bite hard.

Takeaway

Cross-validation made the optimizer's overfitting visible, and swapping in CAPM expected returns fixed most of it. Theory paid for itself: the model with a stronger prior structure beat the model that trusted noisy sample means.
