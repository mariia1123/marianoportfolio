Financial Metrics Analysis

View PDF

Goal

Take a raw Compustat dump of 142,441 firm-year observations, clean it into something analytically useful, engineer the standard financial ratios, and end with a predictive model for the price-earnings ratio.

Dataset

Compustat extract with 18 columns including total assets, equity, net income, earnings per share, and price data. Filtered to active firms in 2007 and 2008 (so we're looking at the financial crisis window), then dropped rows with missing or invalid values on the variables that mattered. That left 6,353 observations.

Variables Engineered

  • MTB: market-to-book ratio
  • Size: log of total assets
  • EPS: earnings per share, with zero values replaced by 0.01 to avoid divide-by-zero downstream
  • PE: price-earnings ratio
  • INV: change in size as a proxy for investment
  • ROA: return on assets
  • ROE: return on equity

Missing EPS and PE values were imputed from related variables (net income over shares, or EPS over price) so we could keep more observations.

Outlier Detection

Used Z-scores on MTB, ROA, ROE, and PE. Any observation with an absolute Z-score above 3 on any of these was dropped. That removed about 70 firms, ending at 6,283 observations. The mean of MTB jumped from 5.55 to 2.46 after outlier removal, and the mean of ROA went from −21.7 to −1.21, which tells you how much the original means were being pulled around by a handful of extreme values.

Predictive Modelling

The final goal was to predict each firm's PE ratio from its other characteristics. We compared four approaches using mean squared error on the training set.

PE ratio distribution after outlier removal. Two notable shapes: a sharp spike near zero (firms with near-zero earnings) and a roughly normal cluster centred around 10 to 15. This shape is what the random forest has to learn.
MSE comparison (lower is better):
Linear regression with winsorized variables, 6971
Linear regression with ranked variables, 6984
Decision tree, 5772
Random forest, 2546

The random forest cut MSE by more than half compared to the linear models. The non-linear interaction terms it picks up on are doing real work that linear models can't capture, even after careful variable engineering.

Takeaway

Most of the work was in the cleaning and feature engineering, not the modelling itself. Once the variables were sensible and outliers were handled, the choice of model still mattered a lot, and tree ensembles clearly won on this kind of cross-sectional financial data.
