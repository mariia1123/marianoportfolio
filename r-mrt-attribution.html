MRT — Attribution Modelling

Group project · RStudio · Sponsored search experiment · March 2023

Group of 3Field experimentMultiple regressionMarketing analytics

Goal

Solve the marketing attribution problem for a medium-sized online retailer: how much website traffic does sponsored search advertising on Google actually cause? The retailer ran a field experiment turning Google sponsored ads on and off across 40 weeks — 20 weeks ON (control) and 20 weeks OFF (treatment) — to isolate the causal effect.

Why an experiment?

  • Causal vs correlational: observational data confounds traffic and ad spend; a true on/off experiment isolates the lift
  • Substitution effect: when sponsored ads go off, organic traffic may rise — only the experiment exposes that
  • Cross-channel comparison: Bing acts as a natural benchmark to weigh whether Google spend is well-allocated
  • Calibrates attribution models: the experiment provides ground truth that retrospective attribution rules can be validated against

The model

I built a new TotalGoogle variable as the sum of organic and sponsored Google traffic, then ran:

TotalGoogle ~ SponsoredGoogleOn + SponsoredTrafficBing + OrganicTrafficBing

The dummy SponsoredGoogleOn equals 1 in control weeks and 0 in treatment weeks, so its coefficient is the causal lift from running ads.

The model explains roughly 73% of the variation in Google traffic — strong for a marketing field experiment with only 40 weekly observations.

Headline result

Sponsored search on Google generates ~4,626 additional weekly visits when turned on. That's the causal effect after controlling for cross-channel Bing traffic — and it's highly statistically significant (p < 0.001).

What's missing for an ROI verdict

Lift alone doesn't answer "should we keep spending?" To compute ROI we'd also need:

  • Cost per click on the Google sponsored campaign during the control weeks
  • Visit-to-conversion rate — how many of those 4,626 extra visits became customers
  • Average order value × profit margin on conversions

With those numbers, we could compare incremental profit against the campaign cost and decide whether to scale, hold, or kill it.

What I learned

This case made attribution intuitive in a way that retrospective last-click attribution never does. The on/off experiment is messy and expensive — but it's the only way to actually know what your ad spend is buying you. Last-click models give the right number only by accident.
