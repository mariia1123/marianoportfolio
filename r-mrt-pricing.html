MRT — Pricing for NPD

Group project · RStudio · Demand modelling · March 2023

Group of 3Demand-based pricingGabor-GrangerProfit optimisation

Goal

Find the profit-maximising price for Robocab's airport-to-hotel ride (16 km) using two demand-estimation approaches — the Gabor-Granger method and a willingness-to-pay (WTP) survey — then derive the optimal price analytically.

Pricing approaches reviewed

  • Cost-oriented: markup over manufacturing/distribution cost
  • Demand-oriented: driven by what customers will actually pay — captures value, lets margins flex with demand
  • Competition-oriented: match the going market rate
  • Value-oriented: price against perceived customer value

The case used demand-oriented pricing because it directly ties the price decision to customer behaviour rather than to internal accounting.

Gabor-Granger demand curve (2016 cohort)

Respondents rated their likelihood to use the service at each price on a 1–5 scale. To correct for hypothetical bias, we converted ratings: 5 → 100% probability, 4 → 40%, everything else → 0%. The resulting curve is cleanly downward-sloping.

Probability of purchase by price (Gabor-Granger)

The main limitation: respondents have to predict their own behaviour in a hypothetical scenario, and they tend to anchor on price alone — ignoring real-world factors like substitutes, mood, or convenience.

WTP-based demand curve (2015 cohort)

The 2015 survey asked respondents their maximum WTP as an open-ended value. We then encoded a binary "would still buy" indicator at each price point (€10 to €25) and averaged across respondents. At €10, ~79% would still buy; at €25, only ~3% would.

Optimal price calculation

With marginal cost of €8 per ride and no competitors at launch, profit is:

Profit = (P × Q) − (8 × Q) where Q = 1.275 − 0.054 × P

Taking the first derivative w.r.t. P, setting to zero, and solving:

Optimal launch price ≈ €14.58 per 16 km ride. This is the price that maximises profit given the estimated demand curve and €8 marginal cost.

Why this matters

The case forced us to reason past gut-feel pricing. Most products are priced wrong because nobody bothered to estimate the demand curve — they just marked up costs or copied competitors. Ten minutes with a survey and a regression beats both.
