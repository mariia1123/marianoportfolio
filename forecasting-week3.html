ARIMA Modeling, Non-Stationarity & Volatility

Supply Chain Forecasting · BMME075 · February 24, 2026

SoloHeating oilGARCH volatilityStationarity tests

Goal

Analyze weekly New York Harbor heating oil prices (2000–2026) using stationarity tests, ARIMA model estimation, and conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) modeling. The assignment demonstrated how to detect and model changing volatility in energy commodity prices, particularly during crisis periods.

Dataset & Context

Weekly heating oil prices spanning 26 years (1,360 observations). The series exhibits persistent trending behavior with major structural breaks: the 2008 financial crisis (sharp decline), the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic (dramatic drop), and 2021–2023 recovery. Volatility is not constant; it clusters around crisis periods and gradually declines during stable regimes.

Key Analyses

  • Stationarity Testing: ADF and KPSS tests on levels and first differences
  • ARIMA Identification: ACF/PACF analysis on differenced series; tested ARIMA(1,1,0), (2,1,0), (0,1,1), (0,1,2), (1,1,1), (2,1,1)
  • GARCH Modeling: Estimated ARCH(10), GARCH(1,1) under normal and Student-t distributions
  • Forecast Evaluation: Fixed-window vs. rolling forecasts over 24-week holdout

  • ARIMA(0,1,1) identified as optimal: MA(1) structure in first differences captures short-term shocks; BIC favors this specification (−4530.9)
  • GARCH(1,1) with Student-t errors superior to normal GARCH: Higher log-likelihood (2465 vs. 2413–2416) reflecting fat tails; shape parameter highly significant
  • Rolling GARCH dramatically outperforms fixed-window: MAE 0.028 (rolling) vs. 0.035 (fixed), reflecting volatility clustering adaptation
  • Squared residuals show AR(3) structure: PACF spikes at lags 1–3 suggest conditional heteroskedasticity is appropriately modeled by GARCH
  • Crisis periods exhibit volatility spikes: 2008 financial crisis and 2020 pandemic show 2–3x normal volatility levels
This analysis reveals a critical distinction: ARIMA captures mean dynamics while GARCH captures conditional variance dynamics. For commodity prices with volatility clustering (energy, metals), ignoring changing variance leads to underestimated uncertainty during crisis periods—a key risk management concern.

Supply Chain Implications

Energy procurement forecasting requires volatility modeling. Standard ARIMA forecasts assume constant uncertainty bands, which is inappropriate when prices exhibit crisis-driven spikes. GARCH models provide time-varying confidence intervals that widen during unstable periods, better reflecting genuine uncertainty in procurement planning.
