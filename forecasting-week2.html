Seasonality, Rolling Forecasts & ARIMA Identification

Supply Chain Forecasting, BMME075, February 17, 2026

SoloSTL decompRolling windowsSARIMA
View PDF

Dutch monthly milk supply from 1995 to 2025 has trend, strong seasonality, and a structural jump in 2015 when the EU dropped milk production quotas. Forecasting it well comes down to two questions: how much history to train on, and which ARIMA fits a doubly-differenced seasonal series.

01The series

Volumes oscillate within each year and peak at year-end. Production trends upward from around 2005, but the biggest shift arrives in 2015, when output jumps from roughly 0.95 to 1.15 kton, aligning with the abolition of EU milk quotas. Protein content shows a similar steady climb, going from 3.59% to 3.64% across recent reporting periods.

To formalize that read, STL decomposition splits the series into trend, seasonal and remainder. The seasonal component is strong and regular, with the same months rising and falling each year. The trend, meanwhile, is flat through the 90s, climbs through the 2000s, and steps up clearly at 2015. The remainder is small, so trend and seasonality together explain most of the variation.

02Choosing a forecasting approach

On a 12-month holdout, additive Holt-Winters beats STL with ETS across every metric, RMSE 39.5 vs 43.7 and MAPE 2.6% vs 2.9%, with MASE, Theil's U and ACF1 all pointing the same way. As a result, Holt-Winters becomes the engine for the rolling-window experiment that follows.

03Window size matters

The next question is how much history to train on. Using an 18-month holdout, a fixed training window gives RMSE 29.6, by far the worst result. Refitting after each new observation, however, drops RMSE to 19.0 and MAPE to 1.26%. So most of the improvement comes from the act of updating, not from a different model.

Sliding windows from 30 to 60 months sharpen this further. Short windows (30, 36) still produce high errors, similar to the fixed case. Performance then improves sharply from 42 months onward, hitting a minimum at 54 months (RMSE 14.6, MAPE 0.99%), before deteriorating slightly at 60. Roughly 4.5 years balances seasonality coverage with recency.

Holdout RMSE by training-window approach
A clean U-shape across window sizes, with the minimum at 54 months.

04Identifying an ARIMA structure

The raw series is non-stationary (ADF p-value 0.084). One-month differencing removes the trend but leaves seasonality intact. Twelve-month differencing removes seasonality but not the trend. Combined (1 + 12), however, the series finally flattens around zero.

From there, the ACF and PACF of this doubly-differenced series both spike at lag 1 and lag 12, pointing to MA(1) plus seasonal MA(1) terms. Four candidate models naturally fall out:

ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)[12] ARIMA(0,1,1)(1,1,1)[12] ARIMA(1,1,0)(0,1,1)[12] ARIMA(1,1,1)(1,1,0)[12]

The first is the cleanest read from the diagnostics, since both ACF and PACF die out quickly after their respective spikes.

Supply Chain Note

In dairy, planning decisions span years (herd size, quotas), but forecasts shouldn't. A 54-month window balances stability with adaptability, enough history for seasonal cycles, recent enough to absorb regime shifts like the 2015 quota removal. As a result, updating regularly matters more than fitting the perfect functional form.
