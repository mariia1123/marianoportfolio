Seasonality, Rolling Forecasts & ARIMA

Supply Chain Forecasting · BMME075 · February 17, 2026

SoloMilk supplySeasonal decompositionARIMA identification

Goal

Analyze monthly Dutch milk supply data (1995–2025) through seasonal decomposition, rolling forecasts with varying window sizes, and ARIMA model identification. The assignment demonstrated how incorporating recent information through rolling windows improves forecast accuracy and how to identify appropriate ARIMA specifications via ACF/PACF analysis.

Dataset & Context

Monthly milk supply volume and protein content for The Netherlands spanning 30 years (360 observations). The data exhibits a clear upward trend since 2005, with pronounced seasonality peaking at year-end. A structural break occurred around 2015 following the EU abolishment of milk production quotas, after which output increased from 0.95 to 1.15 kton.

Key Analyses

  • Seasonal Decomposition (STL): Separated observed series into trend, seasonal, and remainder components
  • Forecast Comparison: Evaluated additive Holt-Winters and STL+ETS on 12-month holdout
  • Rolling Windows: Tested fixed, rolling horizon, and sliding windows (30–60 months) over 18-month test period
  • ARIMA Identification: Applied differencing and ACF/PACF to identify candidate models

  • Rolling forecasts dramatically outperform fixed windows: Rolling horizon achieved RMSE of 19.0 vs. 29.6 for fixed window (36% improvement)
  • 54-month sliding window optimal: Balances information recency with sufficient historical context; further increases degrade performance due to overfitting
  • Additive Holt-Winters superior to STL+ETS: Lower RMSE (39.5 vs. 43.7) and MAPE (2.6% vs. 2.9%)
  • ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)[12] identified as suitable: First-order and seasonal differencing achieve stationarity; ACF/PACF show MA(1) and seasonal MA(1) spikes
The analysis reveals a fundamental principle: updating models with recent observations captures structural shifts and parameter drift better than fixed-sample estimation. For dairy demand, this is critical given seasonal shifts and long-term productivity improvements.

Supply Chain Implications

In supply chain forecasting, production planning benefits from rolling forecasts that adapt to recent demand patterns. A 54-month window represents ~4.5 years of history—sufficient to capture multi-year cycles (policy changes, quota adjustments) while remaining responsive to recent trends. This balances stability against adaptability.
